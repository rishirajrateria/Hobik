> **Quick answer:** A house is effectively a **teardown** when buyers in that area pay mainly for the **lot** and plan to demolish and rebuild. Appraisers analyse the property's **highest and best use** — if a new custom home is the most valuable use, the existing house may add little, and value is driven by lot size, frontage, zoning and comparable lot sales.

## Where teardowns are common

Established neighbourhoods with large lots and strong demand for new custom homes — for example parts of North York (Willowdale, Lansing, Bayview Village), Etobicoke and older areas of York Region — often see older bungalows bought for redevelopment.

## Highest and best use

Appraisers consider what use of the property is:

1. **Legally permitted** (zoning, heritage, conservation rules)
2. **Physically possible** (lot size, shape, slopes)
3. **Financially feasible**
4. **Most valuable**

If rebuilding wins, the property's value approaches the value of the land as if vacant, less demolition costs.

## What drives lot value

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Frontage | Wider lots allow larger, more appealing homes |
| Depth and size | More buildable area |
| Zoning and coverage limits | Determine how much can be built |
| Street and location | Premium streets command premium lots |
| Constraints | Trees, ravines, easements, heritage status |

## Renovating a teardown

Renovations in a teardown market may add little, because buyers plan to remove the house. Talk to an appraiser before spending heavily.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a house be worth less than the land?
In effect, yes — when demolition costs exceed the house's contribution.

### Does as-of-right multiplex zoning affect teardown value?
It can, if the market prices in multi-unit redevelopment potential. Evidence varies by area.

### How do appraisers find lot sales?
They use sales of vacant lots and teardown properties where the buyer's intent to rebuild is clear.
