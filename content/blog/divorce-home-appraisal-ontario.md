> **Quick answer:** In an Ontario separation, the matrimonial home is usually valued as of the **valuation date** — typically the date of separation — for calculating net family property, and often again at a current date if one spouse is buying out the other. A single neutral appraisal addressed to both spouses is usually the fastest, cheapest way to agree on the number.

## Why the home is treated differently

Ontario's equalization system compares each spouse's net family property at the valuation date. Normally you can deduct property you brought into the marriage. The **matrimonial home** is the big exception: if you owned it on the date of marriage and it's still the matrimonial home on the valuation date, you generally can't deduct its date-of-marriage value. That makes its valuation-date value especially important.

*Family law has nuances — always confirm how the rules apply to you with a family lawyer.*

## Two dates you may need

| Date | Used for |
|---|---|
| **Valuation date** (usually separation) | Net family property and equalization |
| **Current date** | Buy-out price, sale planning, refinancing |

If you separated two years ago and are only now negotiating, you'll likely need both. An appraiser can provide them in one engagement.

## One appraisal or two?

Each spouse hiring their own appraiser often produces two numbers that are far apart, followed by negotiation over which one is right — and sometimes a third appraisal. Many lawyers and mediators instead recommend a **joint retainer**: one neutral appraiser, a report addressed to both parties, and the cost shared.

## What makes a divorce appraisal defensible

- Clear valuation date(s) and intended use stated in the report
- Comparable sales from around the valuation date
- The home's condition as of that date (renovations after separation shouldn't inflate the earlier value)
- Transparent adjustments that both lawyers can follow
- An appraiser available to explain or testify if needed

## Buying out your spouse

If one spouse keeps the house, the buy-out normally starts with the **current market value**, then accounts for the mortgage, any agreed adjustments and costs. The person staying will usually need to refinance — and their lender will order its own appraisal. Getting an independent number first helps avoid surprises. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do we both have to agree on the appraiser?
No, but agreeing on one neutral appraiser is usually faster and cheaper than duelling reports.

### Can the appraiser value the home as of our separation date years ago?
Yes. Retrospective appraisals use sales from that period and evidence of the home's condition at the time.

### My spouse won't allow access to the house. Can it still be appraised?
An exterior-only appraisal is possible, with clearly stated assumptions. Your lawyer can also seek access through the process.

### Will the appraiser take sides?
No. Appraisers must be impartial under CUSPAP. The report is an independent opinion of value, not advocacy.
