> **Quick answer:** In Ontario, a co-owner can ask the court for an order to **partition or sell** a jointly owned property under the *Partition Act*. Courts generally favour allowing a co-owner to exit unless there's a strong reason not to. An independent appraisal is central — it sets a fair buy-out price or a sensible reserve for a sale.

## Common situations

- Siblings inherit a house and one wants to keep it
- Unmarried partners separate (the family law rules for married spouses don't all apply)
- Friends or relatives bought an investment property together
- One co-owner lives in the property and won't leave

## How it usually unfolds

1. **Negotiation** — one owner offers to buy out the other at an agreed value
2. **Independent appraisal** — to settle disagreements over price
3. **Application to court** if no agreement — asking for an order for sale
4. **Court-supervised sale**, with proceeds divided according to interests and adjustments

## Why the appraisal is so important

- Gives both sides a neutral starting point for a buy-out
- Helps the court set terms (listing price, reserve, timing)
- Can include **current** and **retrospective** values if contributions over time are disputed

## Adjustments the court may consider

- Who paid the mortgage, taxes and major repairs
- Occupation rent if one co-owner lived there exclusively
- Improvements that increased value

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a court refuse a sale?
Courts have discretion and may refuse in limited circumstances, such as bad faith or oppressive behaviour. Get legal advice.

### Should we each hire an appraiser?
A single, jointly retained appraiser is usually faster and cheaper. If trust is low, each side may hire their own and let the lawyers compare.

### Will the appraiser testify?
If needed. Ask whether the appraiser provides expert testimony.
