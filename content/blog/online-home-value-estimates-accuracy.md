> **Quick answer:** Online estimates (automated valuation models) can give a quick ballpark, especially for common homes in active markets. But they can't see **condition, renovations, layout, secondary suites, lot issues or views**, and they're often less reliable for unique or recently renovated properties. For lending, legal, tax or estate decisions, use a certified appraisal.

## How automated estimates work

They use statistical models built on:

- Public records and past sales
- Listing data
- Property characteristics on file (often outdated)
- Area price trends

They don't inspect the home and usually don't know what's changed since it last sold.

## Where they struggle

| Situation | Why |
|---|---|
| Recently renovated homes | Upgrades aren't in the data |
| Homes with legal suites | Income potential isn't captured |
| Unique or custom homes | Few true comparables |
| Condos | View, floor, exposure and parking vary a lot |
| Teardown areas | Land value drives price |
| Rural or large lots | Sparse sales data |

## When an online estimate is fine

- Early curiosity
- Tracking broad trends over time
- Rough planning before talking to professionals

## When you need an appraisal

- Refinancing or private lending
- Estates, divorce, CRA matters
- MPAC appeals
- Buying firm or selling privately
- Any dispute

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do different websites give different values?
They use different data and models. A big spread is a sign the estimate is uncertain.

### Do lenders ever use automated valuations?
Some do for low-risk files. Many still require a full appraisal.

### Is an appraisal much more accurate?
It's based on an inspection, verified comparables and professional judgement under standards — and it's signed by an accountable professional.
