> **Quick answer:** The **principal residence exemption** usually makes the gain on selling your home tax-free — but you must **report the sale** on your tax return, and the exemption may only partly apply if you owned two properties, rented part or all of the home, or changed its use. In those cases, a value at a key past date (such as a change in use) can be essential to calculate the taxable portion.

## The basics

- A family unit can generally designate only **one** principal residence per year
- The sale must be reported (designation schedule on your return)
- The exemption formula includes a "one-plus" year, which can help when you buy and sell in the same year

## When it gets complicated

| Situation | Why value may matter |
|---|---|
| Owning a home and a cottage | You choose which years to designate to each — values over time matter |
| Renting the home for some years | A change in use may trigger a deemed disposition |
| Renting part of the home | Depends on extent and structural changes |
| Large lots (over half a hectare) | Excess land may not qualify |
| Moving into a former rental | Change in use rules apply |

## Where an appraisal fits

Your accountant may need:

- Fair market value on the **change-in-use date**
- Values at dates relevant to designation decisions between two properties
- Supporting evidence if the CRA asks

A [retrospective appraisal](/blog/retrospective-appraisal-explained) provides a defensible number for any past date.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to report the sale of my home if it's fully exempt?
Yes. The designation must be reported on your return for the year of sale.

### Can I designate my cottage instead of my house?
Possibly, for certain years. It's a strategic decision — get tax advice.

### Can a basement rental affect my exemption?
It can, depending on the circumstances. Ask an accountant.
