> **Quick answer:** A spousal buy-out usually starts with an independent **current market value** of the home. Subtract the mortgage and agreed costs to find the equity, then apply each spouse's ownership share — and remember the house is often only one piece of the overall equalization. The spouse keeping the home typically refinances to pay out the other, so lender approval and the lender's own appraisal need to line up.

## The basic math

Here's a simplified example, ignoring legal fees and tax issues:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Appraised market value | $1,200,000 |
| Mortgage balance | − $500,000 |
| Equity | $700,000 |
| Departing spouse's 50% share | $350,000 |

In real life, the payment is often adjusted for:

- **Equalization of net family property** — the house is one asset among pensions, investments and debts
- **Notional selling costs** — sometimes discussed because the staying spouse avoids commission
- **Post-separation payments** — who paid the mortgage, taxes and repairs since separation

Your lawyers will sort out what applies; the appraisal anchors the biggest number.

## The right order of steps

1. **Get an independent appraisal** (ideally one neutral report addressed to both spouses).
2. **Talk to a mortgage broker** about how much the staying spouse can borrow.
3. **Negotiate the agreement** through your lawyers or mediator.
4. **Refinance** — the new lender will order its own appraisal.
5. **Transfer title** and pay out the departing spouse at closing.

Doing step 1 first prevents the most painful surprise: agreeing on a price the bank won't finance.

## Why the lender's appraisal may differ

The lender's appraiser works for the lender and may be conservative. If your independent appraisal is well supported, differences are usually small. If the bank's number comes in low, a broker can sometimes try another lender — but you can't simply hand the bank your own report.

## Common pitfalls

- **Using the purchase price or an online estimate.** Neither reflects today's value.
- **Renovating before valuing.** Improvements made after separation can complicate who benefits.
- **Forgetting land transfer tax rules.** Transfers between spouses under a separation agreement are often exempt, but confirm with your lawyer.
- **Underestimating refinancing limits.** Equity take-out rules and debt-service ratios still apply.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do we need two appraisals — one at separation and one now?
Often yes. The valuation-date value feeds equalization; the current value sets the buy-out. One appraiser can provide both.

### Can I keep the house if I can't qualify for the mortgage alone?
Possibly, with a co-signer, alternative lender or a different settlement structure. Speak with a broker early.

### Who chooses the appraiser?
Ideally both spouses agree on one neutral appraiser. If not, each may hire their own and the lawyers negotiate between reports.
