> **Quick answer:** When someone dies in Ontario, the executor must report the fair market value of their real estate **as of the date of death**. That value drives the Estate Administration Tax (1.5% on estate value above $50,000) and the deceased's final income tax return, where the CRA treats property as sold at fair market value. A retrospective appraisal from a designated appraiser is the most defensible way to establish it.

## Why the date of death matters

Values change. A home might sell eight months after the death for more (or less) than it was worth on the date of death. Two different numbers matter:

- **Value at date of death** — used for Estate Administration Tax and the deceased's final return
- **Sale price later** — determines any gain or loss the *estate* realises after death

Using the later sale price for both can overstate or understate tax. An appraisal fixes the starting point.

## Where the value is used

### 1. Estate Administration Tax (probate)
Ontario charges Estate Administration Tax on the value of assets that pass through the will: nothing on the first $50,000, then $15 per $1,000 (1.5%) above that. Real estate is valued at fair market value, and only mortgages registered against the property reduce its value for this calculation — other debts don't. After the certificate is issued, executors must file an Estate Information Return within 180 days listing the assets and their values.

### 2. The deceased's final tax return
Under Canadian tax rules, capital property is generally treated as disposed of at fair market value immediately before death. A **principal residence** may be fully or partly sheltered by the principal residence exemption. A cottage, rental property or second home usually isn't, so the date-of-death value directly affects capital gains tax.

### 3. Fair division between beneficiaries
If one beneficiary wants to keep the house, or assets are being divided in kind, an independent value keeps the process fair and reduces family disputes.

## What a good estate appraisal includes

- The effective date (the date of death) clearly stated
- Sales from around that date, not today
- The property's condition as it was at the date of death, using photos, records and family recollection if the home has since changed
- A clear statement of intended use (estate administration and tax reporting)

## Common executor mistakes

- **Using the MPAC assessment.** It reflects 2016 values and isn't a market appraisal.
- **Using an agent's listing price.** It's a marketing figure, not a supported value.
- **Waiting until after the sale and working backwards.** Possible, but harder to support. Order the appraisal early.
- **Forgetting other properties.** Cottages, rentals and out-of-province property have their own rules — ask your lawyer.

## Timeline that works

1. Obtain the death certificate and locate the will.
2. Order a date-of-death appraisal for each property.
3. Give the value to the estate lawyer for the probate application.
4. Share it with the estate's accountant for the final return.
5. If the home is sold, keep the appraisal with the estate records.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an appraisal be done after the home has been sold or renovated?
Yes. Retrospective appraisals rely on historical sales and records of the property's condition at the date of death.

### Does every estate need an appraisal?
Not legally, but executors are responsible for reporting fair market value accurately. An independent appraisal is the strongest evidence if the CRA, the Ministry of Finance or a beneficiary questions the number.

### Is the appraisal fee paid by the estate?
Usually, yes. It's normally treated as an estate administration expense.

### What if the home was jointly owned?
Property held in joint tenancy usually passes outside the estate and may not be subject to Estate Administration Tax, but a value may still be needed for tax purposes. Confirm with the estate lawyer.
